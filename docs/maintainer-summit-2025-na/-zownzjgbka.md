# Summary

In this session titled "Behind the Proposals," OpenTelemetry maintainers Tyler, Robert, Lud, Trusk, and Josh discuss the structure, purpose, and community dynamics of OpenTelemetry Enhancement Proposals (OTEPs).
The panel explains that OTEPs are designed to establish high-level direction and rationale for changes, distinguishing them from specific specification pull requests by focusing on the "why" and consensus-building rather than immediate implementation details.
A key theme is the importance of community engagement, where the group emphasizes that lack of feedback often signals a lack of community pain point, while contentious proposals require active listening, assertiveness, and broad approval to ensure genuine consensus.
As OpenTelemetry matures, the discussion highlights the challenge of managing breaking changes and deprecations, such as the transition from span events to logs, requiring careful migration strategies that balance project evolution with user stability.
Finally, the panel advises newer projects to protect contributor attention by clearly defining project values and processes, suggesting that contributors should start with smaller contributions and build alliances before proposing major design changes to ensure successful integration into the community.

# Main Points

|   # | Main point                                                                                                                                                                       |
| --: | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
|   1 | An OpenTelemetry Enhancement Proposal (OTEP) describes the desired change, rationale, and high-level direction rather than specific implementation details.                      |
|   2 | OTEPs are used when discussions are complex or contentious to focus on the problem and build community consensus before writing specification pull requests.                     |
|   3 | Formal approval requires four green check marks, but practical success depends on engaging experts in the specific area and resolving objections.                                |
|   4 | The community is moving away from a veto culture where the loudest voice wins, aiming instead for broad consensus, especially for contentious proposals like logging and events. |
|   5 | Listening to opposition and understanding the context behind objections is critical for resolving disagreements and building relationships within the community.                 |
|   6 | Lack of feedback from the community or end users often indicates that a proposal does not address a significant pain point, effectively serving as a rejection.                  |
|   7 | As OpenTelemetry matures, design decisions must balance the need for evolution with the stability required by production users, creating a "stability tax."                      |
|   8 | Breaking changes require grounding in actual end user usage data and must provide a clear transition path where the value of the change outweighs the cost of migration.         |
|   9 | Deprecation strategies, such as the transition from span events to logs, are now integral parts of the design process to ensure backward compatibility.                          |
|  10 | Engineering effort should be viewed as an opportunity to solve backward compatibility challenges cleverly rather than as a burden or a tax on the project.                       |
|  11 | Projects should prioritize code that users actually use over "pretty" code, acknowledging that perfect decisions are impossible and evolution is necessary.                      |
|  12 | New projects should establish clear project values and a proposal process to protect contributor attention and manage the volume of incoming ideas.                              |
|  13 | Saying "no" to a proposal should be framed as "not now" to manage expectations, ensuring contributors understand that capacity limits are not a rejection of their ideas.        |
|  14 | Contributors to new projects should start with smaller contributions and build alliances with experienced members before initiating large-scale design proposals.                |
|  15 | The rationale behind design decisions should be preserved and traceable from the proposal through the specification to the final implementation.                                 |
