# Summary

Liz Rice, Bill Mulligan, and Joe Stringer from Isovalent and the Psyllium Project discuss the challenges of establishing effective AI policies in open-source communities, emphasizing that a robust policy must address both the "why" (solving specific project problems) and the "how" (educating contributors).
They highlight that while AI tools can increase the volume of contributions, they often exacerbate issues with code quality, reviewer burden, and the difficulty of verifying a contributor’s true understanding of the code.
The speakers argue that open source is fundamentally built on trust and human collaboration, meaning policies must ensure that contributors can explain, debug, and maintain their AI-assisted work, rather than simply submitting generated code.
Examples from projects like Fedora and Crossplane illustrate practical approaches, such as requiring transparency tags for AI-assisted commits, mandating human final review decisions, and using AI tools to identify correctness bugs while relying on humans to enforce code idioms and project values.
Ultimately, the discussion concludes that the primary goal of these policies should be to foster long-term community sustainability by encouraging genuine learning and engagement, ensuring that AI serves as a tool for upskilling rather than a replacement for human expertise and accountability.
